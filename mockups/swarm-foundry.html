Choices to look at

Biomechanical

The machines look grown as much as built, like the Matrix's machines: rounded carapace with overlapping plates over the frame, exposed ribs rising off their backs, vertebrae where modules stand apart, dark sinew cables sagging in loops, tendrils hanging and swaying, clusters of sensor eyes, horns for smokestacks, hammer legs bent at the knee like an insect's, rounded vats. Ships have domed holds, a head with eyes at the bow and a segmented tail at the stern. Sinew is a cold dark metal, bone a pale one; lights stay the swarm's colour. This holds for every machine from here on.

Ground units (the Units page)

Each cell shows one unit at work in a slice of ground (the last two show cutters working what the specialists leave). Cutters and haulers are drawn as the game draws them; the rest are new.

  • Cutter: small and disposable, in the hundreds. Grey drones, three metres square, in the game's ten shapes, each its own shade (a few run warm or cold), with a light in the swarm's colour and a cargo cell. They fly to the face, cut a block and carry it up to a hauler, working like bees or ants. Cutters eat anything, including what a specialist (sapper's rubble, an abductor's or forester's life, a heat sink's magma) would take more efficiently: the mass taken is the same either way, the specialist is just faster at its own kind.
  • Hauler: the game's twelve-by-nine hull with its small hold, landing on four tentacle legs over the flock. The cutters fill the hold bottom row first; it folds its legs and lifts.
  • Tunneler: an arthropod drill many times a hauler's size (about 60 m long): a segmented, armoured trunk on many legs with a turning drill cone in front. Tentacles reach back from its flanks and dig into the shaft walls to brace it while it bores. The shaft goes downward, never more than 20° off straight down, and it crawls down after its drill toward rich deposits or the magma.
  • Sapper (see also the sapper test planet, its own page): small and sacrificial, carrying a small nuclear charge. It finds a fracture, follows it deep into a cave, arms (its light blinking faster) and goes off hard: a flash, a shock wave racing out through the rock, the ground shaking and the surface heaving over it, a fountain of debris out of the crack and dust. What it leaves depends on what was round it: soft ground (soil, sandstone) is shattered furthest, into fine rubble thrown well outward; hard rock (granite, basalt) breaks into big, irregular pieces that hardly shift, but cracks run out further through it. The blast runs along the open passages and the cave, crumbling the rock beside them further out, and nothing is lost: what crumbles falls into the cave and the passages as rubble. All of it is loose, easy for the cutters. Bigger booms are more fun; on big planets sappers are how hard ground gets broken up.
  • Abductor (living worlds only): a spider on three long legs whose knees ride high above its body. Its head carries a cluster of eyes in the swarm's light colour, glowing low as it roams and flaring, pulsing fast, the brightest flicking from eye to eye, when it has something in its sights; and two fangs; behind a narrow waist hangs its abdomen, a dark, leathery sac in bone hoops that swells as two whip-like grabbers, lashing out and curling back with a barbed hook at the end, stuff live fauna into it. Its laser runs along the ground through whatever runs from it: what it hits bursts, and none of that is collected unless a solid chunk is left for the palps to grab. Blood is a liquid: it soaks into the ground (a reaction for the chemistry table) or runs to low spots and pools, and cutters collect the pools for a hauler.
  • Forester: a crab on three sprawling legs: a wide, flat shell, a crustacean's carapace (higher at the front, a pointed rostrum, spines along the rim, grooves across the plates lit from within, mouthparts under the rostrum that move only while it feeds), two eye stalks on top with tips in the swarm's light colour (they stay above the shell, flicking from one far point to the next and holding, leaning toward the laser's mark or a claw at work, now and then glancing back at the bag or across at each other), a bladed rotor in its intake, and two big claws, saw-edged and systematic (held open as they reach, closed at rest, one clean shear per cut; the intake rotor turns only while it feeds), that go for the larger plants, shearing the trees in sections and lifting them into a woven clippings bag slung behind. Its laser sweeps the ground cover and the small plants at random and sets them alight; fire creeps to what is beside it, and each patch burns down to ash, which the cutters collect for a hauler.
  • Neither roams aimlessly. As soon as it stands it heads for where it is useful, the thickest life about, on a long, fast stride; there it slows right down to work, creeping from one catch to the next; when nothing is left within reach it picks the next place and strides off. (In the game they will cross the surface fast whenever there is nothing to harvest.)
  • Both are dropped from the replicator's gantry, folded into a package of their own shape (a curled spider, its whips coiled under its head and its eyes dimmed; the crab drawn in under its own shell, legs folded flat, claws tucked under the rostrum, eye stalks laid back), fall under gravity, hit the ground, unfold and stand. They have weight: the body hangs on its legs like a load on springs, sagging while a leg is up, jolting as each foot lands under load, surging forward while all three are planted. When a store is full couriers drop from the swarm (several if needed), latch on and haul it up to storage; a new one grows back. Now and then each vents a pressure-release plume, blood (soaking in and pooling) or dry plant matter (for the cutters).
  • Heat-sink bot (a little bigger than a hauler; idea A chosen): a power generator and a coolant in one, turning magma into stone. It hovers over a magma chamber, gripping the walls above it with tentacles where it can, on repulsors otherwise. Its cooling tentacles grow slowly down through the magma toward the bottom, swimming side to side but mostly downward, and the magma freezes to stone round them in broad, solid blocks to be mined. The heat comes up the tentacles to the radiator fins on its back and goes off in great clouds of steam; the power it makes is beamed up to the swarm.
  • After a sapper, after a heat sink (the last two cells): cutters swarm what the specialists leave, like ants. Loose rubble comes away fast; the new stone in broad blocks. Cutters eat anything, specialists' ground included; the mass is the same.

Replicators (the factory, renamed)

A replicator builds ground units from part blocks. It follows the original design: a central hub with the bays hanging off it, each bay an open cradle where a unit is assembled where you can see it, dropping out of the belly when it is done. It is built from the same rules and library as the other machines.

  • Bay types: small 2 (cutters, haulers), medium 3 (plus sappers or heat-sink bots), large 4 (plus the tunneler), super 6 (cutters, haulers, sappers, heat-sink bots, tunnelers, and abductors or foresters on a living world; a lifeless world's super has the first five). Cutters are built in one bank of cradles side by side that grows with the replicator (2 cradles small, 3 medium, 4 large, 6 super), all building at once; every other type has its own bay, and a spare bay builds haulers (large and super).
  • The hub: a blocky rounded body (each its own roundness) with plate seams lit in sequences, a window across its middle where part blocks are sorted past a beating light, and hoppers on its back. A tentacle beside each hopper snatches the incoming couriers out of the air.
  • No diagonals: everything is joined by trusses (two chords, a zigzag web, a bone post every few metres). The biggest bays hang straight under the hub on vertical trusses; the rest hang from a level truss spine run out through the hub to either side. Part blocks ride out along the spine and down the drops. Each bay is a cradle (or a bank of them) between clawed pillars under a beam and a roof of its own shape, caged in front by bone-and-strut bars.
  • Building: cold lasers on a rail under the beam set the unit block by block, bottom up and from the middle out, each new block glowing as it goes on, while clamps hold it. When it is done its lights come on, the clamps let go, it drops out of the open belly and flies off to work.
  • From the shared library: horn stacks, globe tanks, fins, antennas and spines on the hub's back; eyes in the hub; tendrils under it on living worlds; spines on the bays' roofs.

Fabricators

Cold production for an uncaring swarm (the look we are after is Quake 4's Stroggification line). A fabricator turns refined element blocks into part blocks. No particular product is shown, since the game does not model one: what shows is the work done to the blocks.

  • Modules: each its own hull and shape (a flat, domed, gabled, stepped or crested roof; a flat, keeled, rounded, stepped or legged underside), at its own height, in one row (small), two (medium, large) or three (super). Side pods with vats or furnaces hang on struts above or below some.
  • Work cells: each module holds one to four compact cells, each an implement over a pad. A block comes in (dropped from above or pushed in from the side), is worked, and leaves (down through a trapdoor in the pad, or out the side). Each cell keeps its own rhythm.
  • Many ways in and out, no single line: several hoppers on the top row take element blocks from the couriers; several hatches under the bottom row drop part blocks to them. A tentacle grows beside every hopper: it lunges for each courier coming in, snatches it out of the air and drags it into the hopper's mouth. Another hangs under every hatch: it rips each block out as it drops, carries it clear of the hull and lets it go, and the courier flies off from there at its own pace. Tubes join neighbouring modules and run down between rows, with blocks passing through them both ways.
  • Seen through windows: each gallery is closed by a face of hull with ports, tall slots or one long slit; the tubes have small round ports. Most of the work is left to the imagination.
  • Each worked block wears the mark of what was done to it: a laser cut, rivets, drill and spike holes, cable ports lit cold, a copper winding, stitches, a weld seam, frost. Some hatches have a judging eye over them that crushes about one block in five.
  • Implements, picked by the planet's matter weights: drop fist, drop hammer, stamping jaws, restraint clamps, rib clamp (life); laser gantry, core drill, plasma lance (gas), tooth wheel; weld mandibles, rivet head, stitcher, coil spindle, cable coupler; infuser, quench hood, lung torch (gas); many hands (life), grab claw; impaler.
  • From the shared library, on the modules' backs: horn stacks, globe tanks, fins, antennas, spines; eyes set in the hull; tendrils underneath on living worlds.

Nine planets, four machine pages

Each cell is one planet and a machine built for it: a refinery on the Refineries page, a storage ship on the Storage ships page, a fabricator on the Fabricators page, a replicator on the Replicators page. Metallic, rocky, volcanic; living, ocean, fungal swamp; ice moon, hothouse, carbon world. Life works and life grafts appear only on the living world, ocean, fungal swamp and hothouse (a little life).

One parts library for every ship

Every feature is a part in one library, tagged with the matter it works best and where it mounts; a machine draws the parts suited to its job, by its planet's weights. Refineries draw processing parts; storage ships draw from parts made for storing (below).

  • Underneath: hammers (metal), crucible pour (metal), rolling mill (metal), electrolysis bank (fluid), centrifuge (fluid), jaw crusher (ore), soil sifter (ore), fractionating column (gas), cryo condenser (gas), log chipper, digester, rendering press, blood still, bone mill (life); hands (life), muscles (metal).
  • On top: horn stacks (ore), globe tanks (gas), crane (metal), ribs, lungs (gas). Storage ships have a library of their own (see above).
  • On the face: vats (fluid), furnaces and ember grilles (ore), eyes (any), heart, bones, ribcage (life), veins (fluid).

The planet shapes its refineries

Every feature is classed by the matter it works best, and a planet's mix decides what its refineries and ships grow. Pick a planet in the panel at left.

  • Metal: hammers, crucible pour, rolling mill, crane; muscles; bar racks and vaults.
  • Ore: jaw crusher, furnaces, smokestacks, soil sifter; jaws; ore cages and hoppers.
  • Fluid: vats, centrifuge, electrolysis bank; veins.
  • Gas: fractionating column, cryo condenser, globe tanks; lungs; tank rows.
  • Life: log chipper, digester, rendering press, blood still, bone mill; bones, ribcage, heart, hands, skull.
  • Any world: eyes.

Extra jobs, works and holds are picked by those weights; a graft appears with a chance set by its class's weight (certain on the world it suits, never where the planet has none of that matter). Every refinery still has a forge, stacks, vats and a furnace. Life works only where there is life.

Grafts (switch each in the panel at left)

Machine twists on body parts, each a separate switch layered on top of the base design, so any can be removed if we go too far toward the biological:

  • Eyes: big mechanical eyes; the pupil lamp tracks the nearest courier; the lids blink.
  • Bones: metal bones with knuckled ends across hull faces or bracing between modules.
  • Ribcage: bone bars caging the molten vats.
  • Heart: a metal heart in a chamber, thumping a double beat.
  • Jaws: mandibles that chomp at the ore intake; a lower jaw under a ship's bow.
  • Muscles: striated slings between modules that contract and relax.
  • Veins: channels in the carapace with pulses of light running along them.
  • Hands: skeletal metal hands hanging underneath, fingers curling and opening.
  • Lungs: bladders on top that breathe.
  • Skull: on ships, a plated cranium over the bow with sockets and teeth.

Life works (only on planets with life)

  • Log chipper (tree trunks): a trunk slides into a toothed drum; chips spray out; pulp bales.
  • Digester (biomass): a sac of sludge churns and bubbles, bleeding off gas; methane canisters and slurry drums.
  • Rendering press (meat): meat on hooks rides the rail into a press; tallow drips into drums.
  • Blood still (blood): a pump throbs pulses of red round a glass coil; a flask fills and separates; blood drums.
  • Bone mill (bones): bones tumble between rollers; white bone meal falls into a bin.
  • Soil sifter (soil, peat, humus): a sieve shakes; soil falls through; stones roll off the end.

They show on the planets with life (Balanced, Living world, Ocean); in the game they would be built only on bodies whose life allows it.

Built from modules

Each refinery is a cluster of hull modules joined edge to edge, with a dark joint line where two meet. Shapes: box, keel, wedge, drum, tower, pod, and bridges where two modules stand apart. Every module is the same armour, struts and panel lines as the other ships; heat is what sets a refinery apart.

Each module has a job

  • Forge: a row of short drop-hammers under it, each on its own beat; every third blow finishes an ingot.
  • Stacks: 2 to 4 smokestacks on top, flared, crowned, cowled or plain.
  • Vats: 1 to 3 glass vats in its face, each its own molten liquid (iron, white-hot, crimson, toxic green, cyan salt, violet).
  • Furnace: furnace mouths and ember grilles.
  • Globes: round glass tanks of liquid standing on top.
  • Crane: a mast and jib with a swaying chain and hook.

Every refinery gets at least a forge, stacks, vats and a furnace; the rest roll. Plates, rivets, a pipe run, chains, spikes, lamps and the ore hopper go on top of that.

Work hung underneath

Besides the drop-hammers, seven works for solids, fluids and gases. Each refinery hangs a few under its modules; all seven appear across the six shown.

  • Crucible pour (molten metal): heats until it glows, tips on its pivots, pours a stream into a mould; the ingot cools from white to metal.
  • Fractionating column (gases): bubbles rise through its window, amber to pale; a canister fills at the bottom; the side taps puff vapour.
  • Centrifuge (fluids): the rotor spins behind a round window; the sight glass settles into layers; it fills drums.
  • Electrolysis bank (fluids to light metals): electrodes dip into a bath under a copper busbar; blue-white arcs crackle; metal plates come out.
  • Jaw crusher (ore): chunks drop down a chute, two jaws chomp, gravel and dust fall into a bin.
  • Rolling mill (hot metal): a glowing slab goes between two rollers and out as a thin sheet; a shear cuts it.
  • Cryo condenser (gases to liquid): a bellows pumps; frost glitters on an icy coil; drips fill a dewar flask; cold mist drifts off.

Products go to the ships' racks in their own shape: bars for solids, banded canisters for gases, ribbed drums for fluids.

Sizes (buttons along the bottom)

  • Refineries: small 3 to 6 modules; medium 6 to 10, each up to 1.3 times bigger; large 10 to 16 (1.6 times); super 16 to 26 (2 times). Every size grows free-form. Works, hammers, stacks and grafts come with the modules.
  • Storage ships: small about 60 to 88 m long; medium 100 to 150; large 160 to 230; super 260 to 360. Bigger ones have rows of holds with frame bands between, several windows to a row, a thicker frame, more tractor beams.
  • Each window is either one big hold of a single kind of matter or bays split by ribs; the bigger the ship, the more often a big single hold.

Works inside the hull

About two in five works are set into a module instead of hanging under it: a bay opened in the hull, framed in strut, dark and ribbed behind, with the work running inside. A work with no room to hang tries the hull too. Furnace maws and vats were already set into the hull.

(The corridor layout for bigger refineries is shelved: the code is kept in the page, switched off, in case it is wanted later.)

Bigger storage ships: same shape, better fitted

Every size keeps the same hull shapes (lore: one hull design, scaled, is cheaper to build). What grows with size is the fit-out:

  • Medium and up: bulkheads between the holds in one of five styles: ribbed, cross-braced, riveted, vertebral, or lit double rails. Also antenna arrays on the roof and a row of running lights along the top edge.
  • Large and up: a thicker frame, docking clamps on both ends (bigger jaws on super), vent grilles underneath that glow warm, and eye clusters at the lower corners of the hold frame (part of the Eyes graft, so they switch off with it).
  • Super: short separate ribs standing along the roof (no continuous bar) and panel seams on the hull.

Storage ships: holds you can see into

The main feature is that you can see what a storage ship holds. A boxy hull with most of its face one big window, its corners rounded off (no right angles on the outline). The hull is symmetrical: one end shape (square, chamfered, a raked prow, raked back, stepped, or rounded), mirrored on both ends; fittings and grafts may still differ side to side; many ships are two-deck: the hull a deck taller, full length and sharing the ends' shapes, with its own row of holds. The holds fill the hull, leaving just a frame: each row of holds reaches as far as the hull's shape allows at its height, stepping out into tapered ends, so there is no wasted hull. No cabins, bridges or raised blocks: these machines levitate and need no one aboard. The window is split by bone ribs into bays; each bay holds one kind of matter, picked by the planet from the storage ships' own library:

  • Bar rack (metal): bars stacking up as they come.
  • Ore bay (ore): compressed ore heaped to its level, rising and falling.
  • Canister rack (gas): banded canisters.
  • Liquid tank (fluid): the liquid to its level, with bubbles.
  • Organics rack (life, only where there is life): bales and drums.

A little of the UFO stays: rim lights chasing along the bottom, and a tractor beam underneath (lit while couriers load and unload). Biomechanical touches are kept quiet: bone ribs between the bays, a sinew seal round the window, a pair of eyes on the frame, a few tendrils on living worlds. A full bay sends its load on.

Material flow

Each column of refineries is served by the storage ship below or above it. Couriers carry raw ore from the ship's cages and hoppers to each refinery's hopper, and cooled ingots back to its bar racks. Refined bars are 3 m by 2 m, packed edge to edge, brighter than the ore they came from, with a bright top edge and a glint sweeping across the hold now and then. When the hold is nearly full, a load leaves for the fabricators (off the page).

Light

One lighting engine: flames, seams, embers, vats, globes, hot ingots, blows, sparks, lamps and couriers each give off light, laid over the scene in stepped bands. Everything is 1 m blocks: no smooth circles or gradients.

The swarm's light colour is a setting (Lights, in the bar along the bottom: green, cyan, amber, red or violet). Lamps, couriers and the lights in the refineries' plate seams all follow it. Each run of seam lights keeps its own sequence: a light chasing along it, a bar sweeping back and forth, the run breathing, blinking, stuttering, or burning low with a flash now and then. Furnace grilles and ember vents stay the colour of heat.

Open questions

  • Joint lines between modules: keep them (shown), or let modules blend into one hull?
  • Should a module's job show in its shape too (forges always keels, stack modules always boxes)?
  • Storage ship bulkheads: keep all five styles, or settle on one or two as the swarm's standard?
